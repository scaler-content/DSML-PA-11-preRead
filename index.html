The Analyst Files · Case #11
THE 10-MINUTE BET
E-commerce

Setup

You're a Product Analyst at Amazon India. For the Great Indian Festival, Amazon has put Amazon Now, its 10–15 minute delivery service, right inside the main sale. Shoppers can choose quick delivery at search. Leadership asks: “How will we know this is a success? What metrics would you track?”

Evidence

  • The Great Indian Festival started on October 8th, a day before Flipkart's Big Billion Days.
  • Shoppers can now get urgent items in 10–15 minutes without switching to Blinkit, Zepto or Swiggy Instamart.
  • Quick delivery needs local centres and dedicated riders, so each order costs more to deliver than standard delivery.

The Mystery

Before you pick a single metric, you have to set the business goal. What should Amazon's goal be for this launch?

Make Your Move

Choose your first move before continuing. There’s no need to know the answer yet — tap an option to see why it’s worth considering.

Why this move

This is a metric definition question, so the spine of the session is the 4-step framework: Clarify → Business Goals → Define Metrics (AAAERR + guardrails, then North Star / L1 / L2) → Summarize. Guesstimates, cannibalization, A/B testing and RFM plug in along the way.

Your mission: come to class able to explain why quick-delivery orders rising 50% might not mean Amazon gained anything.

Section 2

Real-World Use Cases

Quick commerce vs. e-commerceAmazon, Flipkart and the quick-commerce apps all compete for urgent orders. Analysts must show whether fast delivery brings in new orders or only reshuffles existing ones.
Cannibalization checks on launchesAny new option that competes with an existing one — a new plan tier, a new channel, a new delivery speed — needs a total-orders guardrail before anyone calls it a win.
Partial launch decisionsReal launch calls are rarely a plain yes or no: ship where every guardrail passes, hold where cost or experience breaks, and fix it before scaling.
Product analyst interviews“How would you measure success for X?” is one of the most common interview questions. Interviewers score the order of your thinking, not just the metrics you list.
